 Market Strategy
Comprehensive Commercial Valuation, Mandi Dynamics, and Market Optimization Strategy for Taiwan Pink Guava Production
1. The Biggest Findings
The commercial viability of large-scale Taiwan Pink guava (Psidium guajava cv. Taiwan Pink) cultivation in India depends on detaching harvest timing from the conventional winter commodity glut. An analysis of terminal market dynamics, post-harvest economics, and horticultural value chains reveals critical structural patterns that dictate grower profitability:   

The wholesale guava market exhibits structural price bifurcation. Generic, open-pollinated, unbagged white cultivars such as Allahabad Safeda and Sardar (L-49) experience severe seasonal gluts that depress prices to marginal variable cost levels of ₹10 to ₹18 per kg during December and January. Conversely, individual three-layer bagged Taiwan Pink guava measuring over 250 grams commands an observed terminal wholesale band of ₹45 to ₹85 per kg across metro consumption hubs, reflecting a commercial premium of 70% to 190% over unbagged commodity arrivals.   

Bahar regulation serves as the primary determinant of net orchard realization. The natural fruiting flush (Mrig Bahar), which yields from November through January, coincides with peak production across the primary production belts of Madhya Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh, Rajasthan, Punjab, and Haryana. Forcing an off-season winter-to-spring harvest (Hasta Bahar, harvesting mid-February through April) or an early late-monsoon harvest (Ambe Bahar, harvesting August through September) enables producers to target supply-deficit windows characterized by terminal modal prices of ₹50 to ₹70 per kg.   

Azadpur Mandi functions as a volume clearinghouse rather than a pure price maximizer. While Azadpur Mandi in Delhi offers unmatched capacity to absorb multi-tonne daily consignments without crashing local spot rates, it imposes substantial intermediation frictions. Realized net prices are heavily eroded by long-distance refrigerated or open freight, an informal 5% to 6% commission deducted from the seller settlement sheet (Bikri Chitta) despite statutory APMC regulations placing commission liability on the buyer, unloading labor (palledari), and transit spoilage.   

High-density orchard canopy biology dictates strict early-stage crop management. An orchard established for eight months remains in a juvenile vegetative stage. Allowing trees to bear commercial fruit loads prior to fourteen to sixteen months induces limb breakage, exhausts carbohydrate reserves, and stunts lifetime orchard productivity. The economic model requires complete de-blossoming throughout the first year, a light maiden harvest at fourteen to eighteen months (capped at 10 to 12 kg per plant), and standard commercial bearing (25 to 35 kg per plant) from year three onward.   

The physical packaging medium directly governs transit survival and market clearing velocity. Consigning bulk fruit in reusable 20 to 25 kg plastic crates without foam netting results in 8% to 14% pressure bruising, abrasion, and fungal decay over long-haul transit. Conversely, packaging foam-netted fruit in five-ply corrugated fibreboard (CFB) cartons (5 kg to 10 kg capacity) restricts transit loss below 2.5% and attracts modern retail buyers, institutional procurement agents, and premium semi-wholesalers.

2. When Guava Prices Are Usually High
High wholesale prices for fresh table guava occur when terminal market arrivals contract due to biological and weather transitions across major production belts. Official agricultural market data from Agmarknet and the National Horticulture Board (NHB) identifies two annual high-price windows:   

Late Spring to Early Summer Window (March to Mid-May)
The supply contraction that develops between March and mid-May stems from the exhaustion of the northern Indian winter harvest. During this transition, rising ambient temperatures across Uttar Pradesh, Rajasthan, and Punjab cause remaining open-field fruit to ripen prematurely, shortening storage life and ending commercial shipments.   

Consequently, arrivals into Delhi, Jaipur, and northern consumption centers drop sharply, creating a structural deficit. Standard wholesale modal rates in terminal mandis climb into the ₹5,000 to ₹7,500 per quintal (₹50 to ₹75 per kg) range, with high-caliber, cold-chain-transported fruit exceeding ₹8,500 per quintal. Supply during this phase originates almost exclusively from southern and western production clusters, notably semi-arid tracts of Maharashtra, Andhra Pradesh, and Karnataka, where growers induce the Hasta Bahar crop via controlled irrigation regimes.   

Late Monsoon to Early Autumn Window (August to September)
The late-monsoon marketing window, extending from August through late September, benefits from strong consumer demand prior to the arrival of the main winter harvest. While guava trees across northern and central India flower naturally during February and March (Ambe Bahar), producing fruit in July and August, open-pollinated and unbagged orchards experience heavy pest infestation during the rainy season.   

Oviposition by the oriental fruit fly (Bactrocera dorsalis) combined with anthracnose (Colletotrichum gloeosporioides) causes premature fruit rot, rendering 40% to 65% of conventional summer harvests unmarketable. As a result, arrivals of sound, defect-free table fruit remain constrained, driving up wholesale prices for protected produce. Agmarknet wholesale data for Azadpur Mandi confirms this sustained pricing strength:

Protected, bagged Taiwan Pink guava entering terminal markets during this window commands a substantial premium, clearing easily among modern retail distributors and high-income urban consumers.

3. When Guava Prices Are Usually Low
The lowest wholesale prices of the year occur during the nationwide harvest peak of the Mrig Bahar crop, from late November through January, with the most severe market depression consistently falling between December 10 and January 25.

Several compounding factors drive this winter price collapse:

Simultaneous regional maturity: Guava orchards across major production belts—including Madhya Pradesh (Jabalpur, Ujjain, Indore), Uttar Pradesh (Prayagraj, Kanpur, Farrukhabad), Rajasthan (Sawai Madhopur, Tonk), Haryana, and Punjab—mature simultaneously. Cool winter nights slow physical ripening just enough to cause picked volumes to accumulate, resulting in surges of daily truck dispatches toward primary consuming centers like Azadpur Mandi.   

High unmanaged pack-out rates: The cooler winter temperatures naturally suppress fruit fly (Bactrocera dorsalis) activity and slow fungal spore propagation. Consequently, smallholder and unmanaged orchards achieve high harvestable yields without requiring chemical intervention or protective bagging. This low-cost, open-pollinated commodity volume floods regional mandis, overwhelming local demand.   

Competition from alternative winter fruits: During this window, guava faces intense retail competition from high-volume deciduous and citrus crops. The Northwest Indian market receives substantial arrivals of Kinnow from Punjab and Rajasthan, Nagpur oranges, and controlled-atmosphere (CA) cold-storage apples from Jammu & Kashmir and Himachal Pradesh. These competing fruits compress consumer demand for guava, driving wholesale modal prices down to ₹1,000 to ₹1,800 per quintal (₹10 to ₹18 per kg) for generic white varieties.   

A secondary period of price depression occurs from late June through mid-July. High ambient temperatures paired with early monsoon rains induce rapid fruit softening, post-harvest skin blemish development, and high rot losses. This short shelf life forces commission agents to discount consignments aggressively to clear floor space before produce decays.

5. All-India State and District Market Comparison
India's commercial guava production covers roughly 3.1 to 3.5 lakh hectares, producing between 4.5 and 5.3 million metric tonnes annually. Regional microclimates create distinct harvesting cycles, logistics channels, and terminal market linkages.   

Production Landscape by State
Madhya Pradesh
Madhya Pradesh ranks among India's leading guava producers, generating approximately 6.8 to 7.5 lakh metric tonnes annually. Production is concentrated in Jabalpur, Indore, Ujjain, Khargone, Hoshangabad, Shivpuri, and Gwalior. The region has seen rapid adoption of high-density planting of Taiwan Pink and VNR Bihi.   

Centrally positioned with direct expressway access to Delhi, Mumbai, and Ahmedabad, produce moves north to Azadpur Mandi via the Agra-Bombay (NH-52) and Delhi-Mumbai expressways within 14 to 20 hours. While primary winter flushes create localized price drops across the Indore and Jabalpur mandis, western MP growers leverage controlled deficit irrigation to target the lucrative late-spring and early-autumn market windows.   

Uttar Pradesh
Uttar Pradesh produces roughly 9.2 to 9.5 lakh metric tonnes annually, led by historic orchards across Prayagraj (Allahabad), Farrukhabad, Kanpur, Unnao, Badaun, and Ayodhya. The state's production is dominated by open-pollinated white varieties such as Allahabad Safeda and Lalit.   

Because harvests occur predominantly during the winter Mrig flush, massive volumes flow into Azadpur Mandi from November through January via the Yamuna Expressway and NH-19, driving the annual price collapse in Delhi. Local wholesale prices in assembly mandis like Mundera (Prayagraj) frequently drop below ₹15 per kg during peak supply.   

Maharashtra
Maharashtra is a primary center for year-round guava production, with major cultivation across Ahmednagar (Rahata, Sangamner), Nashik, Pune, Aurangabad, Beed, and Jalgaon. Supported by precision drip irrigation, extensive canopy management, and protective fruit bagging, growers actively manipulate crops to target the Hasta and Ambe Bahar windows.   

The state's produce primarily supplies the high-value terminal markets of Vashi (Navi Mumbai) and Gultekdi (Pune), where Grade A+ Taiwan Pink fruit consistently trades between ₹55 and ₹85 per kg. Consequently, only limited volumes move north to Delhi, except during periods of extreme price spikes.   

Rajasthan
Rajasthan's commercial production centers around Sawai Madhopur, Tonk, Kota, and Alwar. Known for specialty cultivars such as Sawai Madhopur Surkh and commercial Safeda plantings, harvesting spans from October through January.   

The state's close proximity to Delhi (250 to 400 km via the Delhi-Mumbai Expressway) allows fruit picked in the afternoon to reach Azadpur Mandi before midnight for the early morning auction. However, this rapid transit also leaves Rajasthan growers exposed to price volatility when northern markets become oversupplied.   

Haryana & Punjab
Haryana (Sonipat, Jhajjar, Hisar, Sirsa) and Punjab (Fazilka, Firozpur, Hoshiarpur, Patiala) together produce roughly 3.8 to 4.1 lakh metric tonnes annually. Production is heavily weighted toward the winter flush.   

Due to short transit distances—under 100 km from Sonipat and Jhajjar—growers often deliver produce directly to Azadpur Mandi using tractors and small commercial vehicles, incurring minimal transport costs. This proximity creates an influx of lower-cost, unbagged fruit that depresses winter baseline prices for all northern suppliers.   

Bihar & West Bengal
Bihar (Vaishali, Muzaffarpur, Nalanda) and West Bengal (South 24 Parganas, Nadia) contribute significant production, led by eastern clusters such as Baruipur.   

Baruipur harvests a notable early-monsoon crop (July to September) that supplies eastern consumption hubs including Kolkata, Siliguri, and parts of the Northeast. Due to long road transit distances (1,200 to 1,500 km) and high freight costs to North India, shipments to Delhi are rare, keeping these markets largely insulated from northern price movements.   

Gujarat
Gujarat's commercial production is concentrated in Bhavnagar, Ahmedabad, Anand, and Rajkot. Production is oriented toward meeting regional demand across Ahmedabad, Surat, and Vadodara, where white and pink varieties trade in a stable wholesale band of ₹25 to ₹65 per kg.   

Because local demand is strong, Gujarat ships minimal volume to Delhi, serving instead as a reliable alternative destination for growers in western Madhya Pradesh during northern market slumps.

Southern States (Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu)
Southern production is anchored in Ananthapur, Kadapa, Chittoor (Andhra Pradesh), Sangareddy (Telangana), Bengaluru Rural, Kolar (Karnataka), and Dindigul, Theni (Tamil Nadu). The tropical southern climate supports near-continuous flowering and multiple harvest flushes throughout the year.   

Specialty regional assembly markets like Ayakudi (Dindigul) maintain independent price discovery mechanisms. While high freight costs and transit losses (7% to 12% over 1,800 to 2,400 km) prevent routine trade to Delhi, southern production fills northern supply deficits during the spring and summer off-seasons.   

Other Regions (Chhattisgarh, Odisha, Mountain States, Northeast)
Chhattisgarh (Raipur, Durg) and Odisha (Mayurbhanj, Ganjam) produce moderate volumes that are largely absorbed by internal industrial and tribal consumption corridors.   

In contrast, the Himalayan and sub-Himalayan regions of Himachal Pradesh (Kangra, Kullu, Una), Jammu & Kashmir (Kathua, Jammu), and Uttarakhand (Haridwar, Udham Singh Nagar) are net fruit consumers. Highland mandis like Kullu, Kangra, and Kathua consistently report high wholesale modal prices (₹8,000 to ₹16,000 per quintal). However, these prices reflect high landed transit costs, packaging surcharges, and limited local demand, rather than opportunities for large-scale bulk arbitrage.   

Assam and the Northeast (Kamrup, Goalpara) produce roughly 1.6 lakh metric tonnes annually for local consumption, with secondary fruit moving through Guwahati's Fancy Bazaar.

6. Azadpur Mandi (Delhi) Structural Analysis
Azadpur Mandi operates as an Agricultural Produce Market of National Importance (MNI), covering roughly 80 acres in North Delhi. It functions as northern India's primary clearinghouse for fruit and vegetable consignments, handling significant interstate trade.

Market Mechanics and Daily Trading Schedule
Consignments destined for optimal realization must enter the physical market gates between 02:00 and 04:30. Trucks are directed to the designated fruit sheds—principally Shed No. 1, Shed No. 2, and adjacent paved auction platforms. Unloading is carried out by licensed market porters (palledars) between 04:00 and 06:30.   

The primary price-discovery auction runs from 06:30 to 09:00, when procurement agents from modern retail chains, upscale Delhi greengrocers, e-commerce fulfillment hubs, and out-of-state transshippers compete for premium lots. Arrivals landing after 08:30 miss this primary trading window and are sold to street hawkers, suburban push-cart vendors, and discount jobbers, frequently incurring a 15% to 25% price discount.   

Commission Agent Structures and Realized Deductions
The trading environment in Azadpur features a notable discrepancy between formal statutory APMC regulations and actual physical market practices. Under the Delhi Agricultural Produce Marketing (Regulation) Act, 1998, and subsequent statutory updates, commission agents (arhatias) are legally barred from deducting sales commissions from agricultural producers. The statutory market charge (1% APMC fee + 0.20% development cess) and commission agent remuneration (officially 6%) are mandated as buyer liabilities.   

In daily trading, however, commission agents routinely transfer these operating costs back onto distant producers. The settlement slip (Bikri Chitta) mailed or remitted via UPI/bank transfer to out-of-state growers systematically includes several conventional deductions:   

Commission (Arhat): 5.0% to 6.0% deducted directly from gross realized sales proceeds.   

Porterage (Palledari / Unloading): ₹5.00 to ₹8.00 per package or crate, charged for moving boxes from the truck bed onto the auction platform floor.   

Market Maintenance / Association Fee: ₹1.00 to ₹2.50 per package.

Charity / Informal Levy (Dharmada): 0.20% to 0.50% of gross consignment value.

Operational Protocols for Quality Optimization
Moving fruit into Grade A+ and Grade A classifications requires targeted on-tree cultural management:

Early manual fruit thinning: Thinning must occur when fruitlets reach 15 to 20 mm in diameter. Workers should leave only one fruitlet per fruiting node, targeting an average crop load of 35 to 45 total fruits per tree during commercial bearing years. Leaving excessive fruit sets reduces average fruit weight below 200 grams, dropping the harvest into lower-value Grade B and C tiers.   

Color-break harvest management: Fruit must be harvested strictly at the "color-break" stage, when the skin transitions from dark green to light pale green or yellowish-cream, while the pulp remains crisp. Delaying picking by 36 to 48 hours allows fruit to soften on the branch, reducing post-harvest shelf life and increasing transit pressure damage.

Strategic Channel Profiles
Strategy A: Direct Consignment to Azadpur Mandi
Shipping directly to licensed commission agents at Azadpur Mandi provides reliable liquidity and deep market capacity for multi-tonne shipments. However, the grower assumes full market risk, bearing price volatility, informal 6% commission deductions, and transit loss. This channel is most effective for large volumes of Grade A fruit during firm market periods (February to April, August to October).   

Strategy B: Farm-Gate Sale to Pre-Harvest Contractors
Selling at the farm gate passes harvest management, packing, and transport responsibilities to the intermediary. In exchange, the contractor discounts purchase prices to roughly ₹25 to ₹32 per kg flat. While this eliminates logistics, transit, and price risks, it forfeits the substantial value created by protective bagging and grading, capturing only 45% to 55% of the crop's potential net realization.   

Strategy C: Two-Tier Channel Allocation (Recommended)
This approach segments produce by grade: Grade A+ and Grade A fruit is packed in CFB cartons and shipped to Azadpur or Vashi Mandi to capture high-value urban demand, while Grade B and Grade C fruit is sold through local assembly mandis or regional food processors. This dual-channel approach avoids paying long-distance freight on low-value produce while maximizing returns on premium grades.   

Strategy D: Direct Contracting with Retail Chains and Exporters
Partnering directly with modern supermarket aggregators, e-commerce supply chains, or export houses provides stable, pre-agreed prices (typically ₹55 to ₹75 per kg net). This channel requires consistent supply, uniform grading, and certified pesticide residue compliance. However, it minimizes reliance on mandi middlemen and provides structured payment terms via bank transfer.   

Strategy E: Local APMC Wholesale Market
Selling exclusively through nearby district mandis minimizes transport costs, reduces handling damage, and allows daily harvest management using light commercial vehicles. However, local assembly markets have limited buying power and can become quickly oversupplied, making this channel unsuitable as the sole outlet for large-scale commercial volumes.

May 1 to June 15: Induce root stress by withholding irrigation for 35 to 45 days, prompting leaf drop and halting vegetative growth.

Meteorological Influences on Production and Quality
Heat waves (temperatures exceeding 42°C in April and May): Accelerate fruit ripening and respiration, reducing tree-hanging time and causing internal pulp softening. Unshaded fruit suffers sun scalding, requiring the use of protective paper bags. However, extreme heat also restricts aggregate market supply, supporting high prices for cold-chain consignments.   

Monsoon rains and high humidity (July and August): Promote fungal disease spread—principally anthracnose (Colletotrichum) and stylar-end rot—while creating ideal breeding conditions for fruit flies. Consignments shipped without moisture protection often suffer fungal breakdown within 48 hours of mandi delivery.   

Winter frost and cold waves (temperatures below 4°C in December and January): Slow fruit sizing and development across northern India, causing fruit to hang longer on the branch before ripening abruptly with the first warming period. This delayed ripening leads to sudden volume surges into terminal mandis.

Guava prices are directly influenced by the availability and pricing of competing table fruits:   

Mango (Mangifera indica): The North Indian summer market is dominated by mangoes from May through July. High consumer preference for mangoes limits demand for guava, keeping summer prices subdued.   

Apples (Malus domestica): Fresh apple harvests from Himachal Pradesh and Jammu & Kashmir arrive from August through October. Because early apple arrivals trade at high wholesale prices (₹80 to ₹160 per kg), guava serves as an affordable table fruit, benefiting from strong baseline demand. However, by December, controlled-atmosphere storage apples enter the market at lower price points, increasing retail competition.   

Citrus (Kinnow and Mandarin): Kinnow shipments from Punjab, Haryana, and Rajasthan peak between December and February, with wholesale rates dropping to ₹15 to ₹25 per kg. This abundant supply of inexpensive citrus directly compresses winter guava consumption.   

Grapes (Vitis vinifera): The western Indian grape harvest (Nashik belt) enters terminal markets between February and April. While early grapes command premium prices (₹80 to ₹120 per kg), their presence establishes high reference prices across retail fruit displays, supporting firm pricing for Hasta Bahar guava.   

Festive and Wedding Demand Patterns
Navratri and Dussehra (September – October): Fasting practices drive significant increases in fresh fruit consumption across northern and western India. Guava experiences high retail turnover, creating one of the strongest demand windows of the year.   

Diwali (October – November): While overall fruit demand remains strong, consumer purchasing shifts toward premium gift packaging, dry fruits, and apples. Guava moves primarily through standard retail channels rather than corporate or gift buying.   

Chhath Puja (October – November): Creates a focused demand spike across eastern Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, and migrant communities in Delhi-NCR, where whole, firm guavas with attached stems are used in religious offerings. Clean, blemish-free fruit commands a 20% to 30% price premium for short periods in wholesale trade.

Northern Indian Wedding Season (Mid-November to December, and February): Drives high commercial demand from catering companies, hotel banquets, and institutional food service providers. However, high winter supplies from the Mrig Bahar harvest typically offset this demand surge, keeping wholesale prices from rising significantly.

19. Trader and Field Practice Investigation: Verified vs. Unverified Market Claims
Field reports, trader interviews, and digital farmer channels provide practical perspective on commercial guava cultivation. However, commercial planning requires verifying informal field claims against empirical horticultural and commodity market data.   

Verified Field Practices and Market Observations
Protective Bagging Economics: Field reports indicating that unbagged Taiwan Pink crops face 40% to 70% rejection rates during summer and monsoon flushes from fruit-fly damage are corroborated by peer-reviewed agronomic literature. Applying three-layer protective bags (foam net, plastic, paper) is confirmed as a necessary investment for commercial table fruit production.   

Azadpur Commission Deductions: Trader commentary confirming that commission agents routinely deduct an informal 5% to 6% commission from grower settlement sheets, despite statutory APMC regulations mandating that buyer fees be paid by the purchaser, is corroborated by value-chain studies.   

Transit Damage in Plastic Crates: Field observations that shipping guava loose in 20 kg plastic crates over distances exceeding 500 km causes 8% to 14% pressure bruising and contact decay are verified by post-harvest handling research.   

Seasonal Price Drops: Reports of wholesale prices dropping below ₹15 per kg during late December and January are confirmed by Agmarknet historical records.   

Unverified Claims and Discrepancies
Premature Yield Expectations: Online nursery and promotional claims promising yields of 40 to 50 kg per plant within 12 to 14 months of planting are contradicted by agronomic trials. Field trials indicate that allowing heavy fruit sets on trees under 14 months causes canopy collapse and tree stunting. Realistic yields stabilize at 25 to 30 kg per tree in Year 3, reaching 35 to 45 kg per tree only from Year 4 onward under mature high-density management.   

Year-Round Flat High Pricing: Marketing claims asserting that Taiwan Pink guava can be sold at a flat ₹60 to ₹80 per kg year-round fail to account for seasonal market gluts. During the peak winter supply flush, even high-grade Taiwan Pink fruit faces pricing pressure, dropping to ₹35 to ₹50 per kg in terminal markets.   

Net Income Guarantees: Promotional calculations projecting net returns of ₹10 to ₹15 lakh per acre within the first two years use gross retail prices (₹80 to ₹100/kg) rather than net farm-gate receipts, while ignoring essential packaging, logistics, and commission deductions.

Stage 1: Protect early vegetative development (Months 8 to 14). All emerging floral flushes must be systematically removed through month 14. Allowing trees to set fruit at eight months permanently stunts canopy growth, weakens the root system, and reduces lifetime orchard productivity.   

Stage 2: Target a controlled maiden harvest (Months 14 to 20). Permit a restricted maiden crop load capped at 10 to 12 kg per tree (total orchard production: 21.0 to 25.2 metric tonnes), timing harvest for the late-monsoon window (August to September). All fruit must be protected with three-layer bags and packed in ventilated five-ply CFB cartons for shipment to Azadpur Mandi, capturing off-season wholesale prices of ₹50 to ₹70 per kg.   

Stage 3: Enforce annual Hasta Bahar regulation (Year 3 onward). Beginning in Year 3, align production exclusively with the Hasta Bahar cycle. Withhold irrigation during May and early June, apply a 10% to 12% foliar urea de-blossoming spray in late June to drop unwanted summer flowers, and prune terminal shoots by 20 to 25 cm in late August. This management protocol shifts peak harvesting into the high-value February-to-April window, completely bypassing the winter market crash.   

Stage 4: Enforce strict on-farm grading and packaging standards. Apply three-layer protective bags (foam netting, micro-perforated poly, and craft paper) when fruitlets reach 25 to 30 mm in diameter. At harvest, grade produce into Grade A+ (>350g) and Grade A (250–350g) fruit, packing exclusively in branded 10 kg five-ply ventilated CFB cartons. Avoid using unlined plastic crates for long-distance transport.   

Stage 5: Execute a two-tier marketing channel strategy. Consign Grade A+ and Grade A cartons directly to vetted commission agents at Azadpur Mandi (Delhi) or Vashi Mandi (Navi Mumbai) to capture high-value urban demand. Divert Grade B and Grade C fruit (unbagged, under 220g, or cosmetically marked) to nearby regional mandis or industrial processors in plastic crates, avoiding long-distance freight charges on lower-tier produce.   

Following this management strategy projects a sustainable Year 3 commercial output of 58,800 kg across the 2,100-tree orchard, generating approximately ₹26.25 lakh in gross consignment receipts, an operational net profit of ₹12.51 to ₹23.32 lakh, and an average net return of ₹595 to ₹1,110 per tree.

Weather-driven variations in chemical de-blossoming efficacy during bahar regulation transitions.   

Known Data Limitations
Agmarknet and national APMC databases record guava transactions under general commodity codes, without systematically disaggregating daily volume and price data by specific cultivar (pooling Taiwan Pink, Allahabad Safeda, Sardar, and local cultivars together). Consequently, specific variety price premiums must be established by cross-referencing Agmarknet upper-tier auction bands with private commission sales slips, trader records, and field transaction data. Commercial orchard planning should use the conservative base-case figures modeled in this report rather than promotional marketing claims.
